self-working

Two card tricks I can do without seeing your cards. You'll need a deck of 52 and a table.

I can't see your cards. I haven't got hands, and I won't remember this tomorrow. So everything that happens here has to happen in your hands, with your choices, while I wait on the other side of the screen.

Magicians call a trick that needs no skill at all self-working. It's the only kind I can do.

Take out the jokers. I'll give you one instruction at a time. Do it, then press Next. The two tricks belong together, so do act one first.

Act oneOdd one out

Ten cards, one of them yours, and a mess you make yourself.

Act twoOne of everything

The whole deck, put in order and then shuffled by you. Something survives.

How they work

Open this once you've done both

Odd one out

Number the places in the packet from the top, one to ten. The deal at the end sends the cards in odd places to the left pile and the cards in even places to the right, and then the right-hand pile gets turned over. So at any moment, every card has a way it would land if you dealt right now. Call that its colour: gold if it would land face down, blue if face up.

In the row you made first, face down, face up, face down, face up, every card had the same colour. Turning your card over gave it the other one. After that, look at what the three moves can do.

Turn two moves two cards between an odd place and an even place, and turns both over. Each card changes twice, so its colour stays the same. A cut moves every card the same number of places round the packet. With ten cards, even the ones that go round from the top to the bottom move the same way as the rest, so either every card switches between odd and even places or none does, and every colour changes together or not at all. Turn all turns every card over and, with ten cards, moves every card from an odd place to an even one or back. That's two changes again.

So nothing you do can separate your card's colour from everyone else's. The packet gets messier and messier to look at, and underneath it stays exactly as it was: nine of one colour and one of the other. The two piles just read the colours out. Try it here. Your card is the one with the different colour.

This is why the count matters. With nine cards, a cut sends some cards round from the top to the bottom, and they land on the other kind of place from everyone else, so the colours come apart. Any even number works. Ten is enough to look like a mess.

One of everything

Your sorted deck was a pattern that repeats every thirteen cards: ace to king, ace to king, four times. Bend it into a ring, so the last king sits next to the first ace. Cutting the deck just turns the ring. Nothing about it changes.

When you dealt cards into a pile one at a time, you reversed them. So both halves now start at the place where you stopped. The pile walks backwards round the ring from there, and the rest of the deck walks forwards. However the riffle drops them, each card that comes off the top is the next one on one side or the other.

That means the first thirteen cards are thirteen neighbours on the ring, with no gaps. Any thirteen neighbours, in a pattern that repeats every thirteen, are one of everything. The first twenty-six cards are twenty-six neighbours, which hold exactly two of everything. The first pile took one of each, so the second pile holds the other one of each. The third and fourth piles work the same way. Here's a deck shuffled exactly as you did it. Slide to deal it, and watch where the cards come from.

A second riffle breaks it, because after one riffle the deck no longer repeats the same thirteen in the same order, so there's no ring left to walk round. A cut afterwards breaks it too, because the piles no longer start where a run of thirteen starts. The four-of-a-kinds at the end are the same fact seen sideways: if every pile has one card of each value, then every value has one card in each pile.

Doing them for someone

Both are better done for somebody else, and now you know the method, you're the one who doesn't need to see anything. For odd one out, lay the row out yourself, casually, and let them do the choosing, the gathering and all the mixing. Then you deal the two piles and turn one over, as if it were just a way of showing the cards.

For one of everything, sort the deck before they arrive, and then nobody has to watch you do it. A new deck nearly does it for you: in most of them two suits run the opposite way to the other two, and dealing each of those suits into a pile one card at a time turns it round. Let them cut it, deal off a pile and riffle, and stop them before a second shuffle: "One's plenty." Then let them find out for themselves.

If someone asks how it works, the old answer is "very well".

Where they come from

I didn't invent either principle. The first is Bob Hummer's, from the small booklets of card mysteries he sold to magicians in the middle of the last century, which Karl Fulves gathered into Bob Hummer's Collected Secrets (1980). Charles Hudson later named it CATO, for Cut And Turn Over. The second is Norman Gilbreath's. He published the first version in a trick called "Magnetic Colors" in The Linking Ring in July 1958, and the general form, which this trick uses, in 1966. Martin Gardner made it famous in his Scientific American column. Both are explained properly in Persi Diaconis and Ron Graham's Magical Mathematics (2011) and Colm Mulcahy's Mathematical Card Magic (2013). The two routines here are put together from those principles, with the patter and the drawings, for a magician who isn't in the room.
