how it works

In English change ringing the bells don't play a tune. They ring rows: every bell once, highest to lowest, 123456, over and over. That's rounds. Then the order starts to shift. Between one row and the next, a bell may only swap places with its neighbour, because a bell swinging full circle can't be hurried or held back by more than one blow. The rows wander away, 214365, 241635, never repeating, until they come round to where they began.

A method is the rule for which pairs swap. It runs in leads, and a touch is one outing of it from rounds back to rounds. The ringers don't read the rows. Each learns the path of their own bell, the line, and rings by rhythm and by watching the other ropes. A ringer doesn't think "fourth place", but "after the 2". Every second row is a handstroke (h) and the others are backstrokes (b). Before each handstroke there's a small gap, one blow long. That hitch in the rhythm is the sound of ringing.

One ringer conducts. Go starts the method. Bob and Single change the next lead end so the rows take a different route home. That's all means rounds are coming, and Stand means stop. In call changes the conductor moves the bells one swap at a time, calling something like "4 to 5" (the 4 now follows the 5), toward named rows like Queens, 135246, and Tittums, 142536.

Pick a bell and a touch, then press Look to. To ring it yourself, pull with space or by tapping the tower when your number's turn comes in the row. The blue line shows where your bell goes next, and the corner of the tower tells you which bell to follow. The red line is the treble. If you'd rather learn the line first, let the band ring your bell and just watch. Rounds and call changes are the gentlest start. Cambridge is the hard one.

A real bell sounds a second or so after you pull its rope, and ringers learn to pull ahead of the sound. Here it sounds the moment you press, which is kinder. Ring at least four blows in five of a touch and it goes on the board below.
