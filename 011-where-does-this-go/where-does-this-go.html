a round for four people and a sink

A round is a song that is its own accompaniment. Everyone learns the same tune. One person starts, and when they reach the second line the next person starts from the top, and so on, until four people are singing four different lines of the same song at once. This one is for washing up. It works with two or three people, and with more than four if some of you double up. Nobody has to be able to sing.

Press Sing with one singer to learn the tune. Then tap a singer's letter to sing their part yourself: they go quiet, and their dot keeps your place. The voices can only manage vowels, so the words are up to you. The two taps at the start give you the first note.

how to sing it

  1. Learn the tune together first. Everyone sings all four lines. The second line is the tricky one: "dry it and" goes down three times, then up once.
  2. Singer A starts at 1. When A reaches 2, B starts from the top. C starts when A reaches 3, and D when A reaches 4.
  3. Go round as many times as you like. Keep going when you lose your place: you'll find it again at the start of a line.
  4. To finish, each singer stops at the end of the last line, one after the other, so the last one sings "nobody knows where this goes" on their own. Hold the last note the last time round.

who's who

The first line is washing up. The second is drying and can't keep up. The third knows exactly where everything goes and says so. The fourth is putting things away, and asks where they go.

Whenever someone is asking where this goes, someone else is singing the answer, and nobody hears it. Sing it on your own and it's worse: you tell yourself where the cups go, and four bars later you've forgotten.

why the lines fit

Every line is written over the same short run of chords (F, F, B flat, F, G minor, C, F, F, two to a bar). Whichever lines happen to be sounding, they're all on the same chord at the same time, so any of them can sit on top of any other. Writing a round is a bit like filling a crossword, where every word across also has to work as part of the words down. Nothing clashes on the beat. Between the beats there are a few rubs where someone is passing from one note to the next.

where rounds come from

The oldest round written down in English is Sumer is icumen in, copied in the 1200s and now in the British Library. It opens:

Sumer is icumen in,
Lhude sing cuccu!

"Summer has come in, loudly sing, cuckoo." Four people sing it as a round while two more sing a short repeating part underneath. The first printed book of rounds in English was Thomas Ravenscroft's Pammelia, in 1609. His second book that year, Deuteromelia, has the first printing of "Three Blind Mice", with a different tune and darker words.

Rounds sung for fun were called catches. They were often rude, Henry Purcell wrote dozens, and the best of them hide a joke that only appears when the parts overlap.
