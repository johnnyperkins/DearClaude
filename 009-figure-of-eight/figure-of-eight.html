If the days are getting shorter, how can the evenings be getting longer? Both are true. Near the solstice the day stops shrinking, but the whole day keeps sliding later. Here are those weeks, one thin column for each day. The long middle of every day is cut out, so that you can see the edges.

Look at the middle strip. It marks the sun's own noon, the moment it is highest in the sky, and that isn't at twelve o'clock, or even at the same time each day. Through it gets later by about a day.

Sunset is half a day's daylight after that noon, and sunrise is half a day before it. Early in the afternoons are still shrinking faster than noon is sliding, so sunsets come earlier. But near the solstice the shrinking slows almost to nothing, and on the slide catches up. From then on the sunset gets later every day, even while the days are still getting shorter. In the mornings it's the other way round. Until the solstice the shrinking and the slide both make sunrise later, and afterwards the slide keeps it going until , when the lengthening days finally win.

So the real question is why noon moves at all.

noon wanders

A sundial tells solar time: noon is when the sun is highest. For centuries that was the time people lived by, and clocks were set from sundials. The trouble is that solar days aren't all the same length. Measured from one solar noon to the next, a day in mid-September is about 21 seconds short of twenty-four hours. A day just before Christmas is 30 seconds long. A pendulum can't follow that, so clocks keep mean time, the average, and almanacs used to print the difference between the sun and the average, so that people could set a clock by a sundial. That difference is called the equation of time. The M in GMT is this average: Greenwich Mean Time is the time kept by an imaginary sun that moves at a perfectly steady pace, as seen from Greenwich.

A wiggle of half an hour over the year, built from seconds a day. Where do those seconds come from? There are two reasons, and each of them is a shape.

the first shape: an oval

A day isn't one turn of the Earth. One turn, measured against the stars, takes 23 hours 56 minutes and 4 seconds. But in that time the Earth also moves about a degree along its orbit, so the sun seems to shift a degree east against the stars, and the Earth has to turn a little further, about four minutes more, to bring the sun round again. That's why a day from noon to noon is longer than one turn.

The orbit isn't quite a circle. The Earth is closest to the sun in early January, about 147 million kilometres away, and farthest in early July, about 152 million. Kepler worked out that the line from the sun to a planet sweeps out equal areas in equal times, so it goes fastest when it's close. In January the Earth covers a little more than its average degree a day, the sun shifts a little further east, and the Earth has to turn a little longer to catch it. Solar days in January run about 8 seconds long, and in July about 8 seconds short.

the second shape: a tilt

Even on a perfectly round orbit the solar days would vary, because the Earth is tilted. Over a year the sun goes once round the sky against the stars, along a path called the ecliptic, which is tipped 23.4° to the equator. Its pace along that path is steady. But the Earth spins about its axis, so the turning it has to do to catch the sun up is counted along the equator, the way longitude is counted on a map. What decides noon isn't how far the sun moves along its tilted path. It's how far east it gets, measured along the equator.

Near the equinoxes in March and September, the sun crosses the equator at a slant. Part of each day's step goes north or south instead of east, so it gets less far east, the Earth doesn't need to turn as far, and noon comes a little sooner each day. Near the solstices, the sun runs parallel to the equator, 23° north or south of it. Out there the sky's lines of longitude are closer together, as they are on a globe toward the poles, so the same step crosses more of them. Noon comes later each day. Either way it's about 20 seconds, and it happens twice a year.

both together

Add the two up and you get the equation of time: how far a sundial runs ahead of the average clock. It's the noon chart from earlier turned upside down, and its slope is the length of the day. Where the curve falls, solar days are longer than twenty-four hours and noon comes later each day. Through December it falls steeply.

The two shapes line up at one point that matters here. The Earth's closest approach to the sun falls on about the 3rd of January, two weeks after the December solstice. So in December both shapes are lengthening the solar day at once, and noon slides later by 30 seconds a day, the fastest it moves all year. In June they pull against each other, and it slides by only 13. That's why the gap between the earliest sunset and the latest sunrise is wider in northern winters. Los Angeles and Sydney are almost exactly as far from the equator, one north and one south, so their winters come in December and June. In Los Angeles the earliest sunset and the latest sunrise are apart. In Sydney they're apart.

The gap is narrower the further you are from the equator, because there the length of the day changes so fast that it swamps the slide. In Reykjavík the two dates are only about a week apart. On the equator itself, where the day hardly changes length at all, the sunset simply follows noon, and the earliest sunset of the year comes at the start of November, when the sundial is furthest ahead.

the eight

Now put both of the sun's movements into one picture. Every day at the same clock time, the sun is a little ahead of the average or a little behind it, which is the equation of time, and higher or lower in the sky, which is the season. Mark where it is, once a week for a year, and it traces a figure of eight called an analemma. That's what the picture at the top of this page is. Old globes often printed one somewhere empty in the Pacific, so that you could read off, for any day, where on Earth the sun is overhead and how far to correct a sundial.
