a place in the sun

A game for two, played with coins. Heads joins top to bottom, Tails joins side to side. The sun goes round the board, and every coin you put down throws a shadow that can turn one of theirs over.

    How to play

    1. Toss a coin to see who puts down the first coin. It goes heads up, on any space. The other player then chooses a side: Heads, which makes that coin theirs, or Tails.
    2. Tails moves next. From then on, take turns putting a coin on any empty space, your side up.
    3. The new coin throws a shadow onto the next space, directly away from the sun. If one of your opponent's coins is lying there, turn it over. It's yours now.
    4. The sun has six places around the board, numbered 1 to 6. It starts at 1, in the east. Each time Heads has played, the first coin included, it moves on to the next number, clockwise.
    5. Heads wins by joining the top and bottom edges with an unbroken chain of heads. Tails wins by joining left and right.

    Worth knowing

    Only the coin you just put down turns anything over, and it turns at most one coin. Every coin on the board has a shadow, but the old ones are only telling you where the sun is.

    So to turn over one of your opponent's coins, put yours on the space beside it on its sunny side. And to see whether one of your own coins is safe, look at that same space. If it's filled, or off the board, nothing can be put there to throw a shadow on your coin. Look under the sun your opponent will play by: the same sun if you're Tails, the next one if you're Heads. On screen, a small red mark on the edge of a coin shows it could be turned over on the next move, and from which side.

    The sun goes all the way round every six rounds, so shadows fall each of the six ways in turn. A coin that's safe now may not be safe in two rounds. The sun always moves the same way, so you can see it coming.

    A game can't end in a draw. It's the same as in Hex, which this game borrows its board and its goal from: once every space is full, exactly one player has a chain. Most games are over long before that.

    The first rule is there to make the game fair. Under each new position of the sun, Tails plays first and Heads answers, so Heads always gets the last word. In thousands of games Claude played against itself, that was worth about 60 wins in 100 to Heads, and the first coin pays for it. Put it somewhere strong and your opponent will take Heads. Put it somewhere weak and they'll leave it to you. The fair place is somewhere in between. It's the swap rule from Hex, or cutting a cake for someone else to choose from.

    Hex was invented by Piet Hein in 1942 and again, on his own, by John Nash in 1948. The sun and the shadows are new.

    To play at a table

    You need a board, about forty coins of any kinds, and something to mark the sun: a button, a ring, a sweet. You share the coins. One of you plays whatever lands heads up, and the other plays tails.

    It prints on A4 or Letter, landscape, with room for coins up to about 22 mm. Pennies and cents are ideal. The rules come on a second page.
