the whole street

Ten houses, one evening, the week the clocks go back. Each house has a sonnet, and at line nine the power goes in all of them. Tap a line to hear it from the next house along.

      Line one of any house fits after line two of any other, and so on all the way down, so the street holds 1014 sonnets, a hundred thousand billion. Reading one a minute, day and night, would take about 190 million years. You are almost certainly the first person to read the one above.

      The idea is Raymond Queneau's. In 1961 he published Cent mille milliards de poèmes: ten sonnets printed one to a page, each page cut into fourteen strips so you could turn the lines separately. Here every line has to stand alone. No line leans on someone who was only named in the line before, so a mix never leaves you with a she who isn't there. Each line also has the same job in all ten sonnets. Line nine is always the power going off, and line thirteen is always it coming back.

      The address under the poem lists the house each line came from. You can share it, and it opens the same poem. Tap a house, or a name in the list, to read one house's sonnet on its own.
